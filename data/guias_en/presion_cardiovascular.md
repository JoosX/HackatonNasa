## Why it matters
Blood pressure and pulse show how the heart is working. Large or sustained changes can indicate stress, dehydration, lack of sleep or a cardiovascular problem.

## What happens in space
Without gravity, blood and body fluids shift toward the chest and head. The heart and blood vessels adapt to this new situation, which can change blood pressure and pulse. NASA studies these adaptations because they could increase the risk of cardiovascular events on long missions.

## How to measure on board
For routine blood pressure: rest 5 minutes, use a properly sized cuff on bare skin and do not talk. The position used on Earth and its supports do not automatically carry over to microgravity: follow the restraint and position in the mission procedure. For resting pulse: rest at least 10 minutes and count the beats at the wrist for one minute, or use the monitor according to its manual. Log the context and unit. With urgent symptoms, do not wait these times before asking for help. Extended preparation is in each indicator's guide; technique references: CDC Measuring Your Blood Pressure and MedlinePlus Pulse.

## Warning signs
Blood pressure much higher or lower than your normal, a very fast or irregular pulse at rest, dizziness, chest pain or pressure, shortness of breath or fainting.

## What to do
- Check the device, position and context according to the mission procedure. With chest pain, shortness of breath or fainting, alert immediately without waiting for another reading.
- Hydrate according to the mission plan.
- Check whether there was little sleep, stress or intense exercise recently.
- If the value stays high or low, alert the crew medical officer.
- With chest pain, shortness of breath or fainting: alert the crew immediately and follow the onboard emergency protocol.

## When to alert
Yellow: a value outside your normal that repeats on the second measurement. Red: a very abnormal value together with symptoms such as chest pain, shortness of breath or fainting.
