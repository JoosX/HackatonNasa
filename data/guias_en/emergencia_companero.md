## Why it matters
Strong chest pain followed by fainting can be a heart problem, such as a heart attack or an arrhythmia, or something that keeps enough oxygen from reaching the brain. Nobody on board has to diagnose it: what helps most is acting quickly and in order during the first minutes.

## What happens in space
Far from Earth, medical help can take minutes or hours to answer, or not answer at all if there is no link. The crew is the first and main rescue team. In microgravity the body floats: to help, you have to hold your crewmate and hold on yourself.

## How to measure on board
If your crewmate is breathing, measure pulse and oxygen with the pulse oximeter on a finger and note the time of each measurement. If they are not breathing, do not waste time measuring: CPR and the defibrillator come first.

## Warning signs
No response when spoken to loudly or when their shoulder is tapped, not breathing or only gasping, pale or bluish skin, chest pain spreading to the arm, neck or jaw, heavy sweating, shortness of breath or confusion.

## What to do
- Call out a medical emergency and call the crew medical officer; ask for the emergency kit and the defibrillator.
- Hold your crewmate so they do not float or hit anything, and hold on yourself.
- Speak loudly and tap their shoulder to see if they respond.
- Check whether they are breathing for 10 seconds.
- If they are not breathing or only gasping, start CPR: hard and fast compressions in the center of the chest (100 to 120 per minute) and use the defibrillator as soon as it arrives, following its voice instructions.
- If they are breathing, keep them secured on their side, watch their breathing and measure pulse and oxygen.
- Note the time they collapsed and everything that was done.

## When to alert
Red: always and immediately. A crewmate who collapses or has strong chest pain is an emergency: alert the whole crew and follow the onboard emergency protocol.
